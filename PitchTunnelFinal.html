Tunnel+

Author

Jack Sadowski

Introduction and Prior Work:

When I did my investigation into fastball characteristics and whiff rates one major factor outside of our analysis was how a pitchers fastball interacts with their other pitches. Pitches do not exist in a vacuum. The success of a pitch can be heavily dependent on the profile of the previous pitch. For example Greg Maddux’s individual pitches weren’t overwhelming. But he threw them in a way that they looked the same for as long as possible on their path to the plate, before breaking apart at the last second. This approach, which was wildly successful for Maddux, is known as pitch tunneling. 

Tunneling is very well known in the baseball world, but there are surprisingly few accessible metrics that capture it. There is no Stuff+ or Location+ equivalent for tunneling. 

The main metric I could find that attempted to quantify tunneling was from Baseball Prospectus in 2017 and was a big inspiration for my project. They created something called “Tunnel Differential”, which is the distance between two pitches at the point where the hitter has to make a swing decision. They called this point the “Tunnel Point” and measured it to be about 175 milliseconds before contact which translates to about 23.8 feet before the plate (the tunnel point I used for my own calculations). They also measured the difference at release between several pitches and how much a pitch moves after the tunnel point. 

Another more recent metric that I found is Driveline’s Match+. Match+ essentially evaluates the overall similarity of a pitchers arsenal out of the hand, using continuous 3D trajectory modeling. 

Both of these metrics have immense merit, but they left a gap I wanted to explore. My goal was to create an all-in one metric to evaluate the full flight of individual pitches instead of arsenals. This would then allow me not only see and create overlays of some of the nastiest tunnels but also determine what pitchers actively tunnel the best on an individual pitch basis rather than just have the best tunneling stuff.

Data and Data Manipulation:

Statcast data from Baseball Savant offers incredibly precise pitch tracking data. I was able to download a dataset of all pitches from the 2024 and 2025 MLB season from a user on Kaggle which gave me everything I needed to conduct the analysis. Rather than the computationally expensive and complicated continuous pitch tracking used by Driveline I used a similar approach to Baseball Prospectus. Using kinematics and the release point, end point, velocity, and acceleration data given by Stacast, I calculated each pitch’s position at four discrete points along its trajectory:

  1. Release

  2. Early Flight (40 feet from the plate)

  3. The Tunnel Point (28.3 feet from the plate)

  4. Home Plate

Using these calculated positions I then grouped each pitch by at bat, and then calculated the difference between each pitch and the previous pitch’s position at all four checkpoints.

Dimension Reduction:

Naturally, these four variables have high covariance. A pitch’s position at release is extremely correlated with its position at 40 feet, and so on. To account for this I ran a Principal Component Analysis on the four variables which was something I saw in one of the papers we looked at for my Applied Statistical Modeling class and wanted to try out. It worked exceptionally well. The first two components not only represented 88.05% of the variation, but they perfectly matched real-world pitch qualities:

  • PC1 (Overall Separation): Had entirely positive loadings, with the heaviest weight placed on the distance between pitches at the tunnel point. This component represents the overall separation between the two pitches.

  • PC2 (The Late Break): The loadings for the distance between the pitches at release and at 40 feet were positive, while the loadings for the distance at the tunnel point and at the plate were negative (with the distance at the plate being twice as heavily weighted). This component can be interpreted as the late break of the pitch.

Creating Tunnel+:

Using these two components I then ran a linear regression model predicting delta_pitcher_run_exp (Statcast’s metric that represents the change in run expectancy based on a particular pitch). This is similar to how metrics like Stuff+ and Command+ from FanGraphs are created. Unfortunately the “Tunneling” the model liked didn’t match the conventional definition of tunneling. The coefficient for PC1 was positive and significant indicating that the model preferred pitches with low separation, however the coefficient for PC2 was positive, and since the loadings for the distance between the pitches at the tunnel point and at the plate were negative this meant that the model preferred pitches with low late break. 

Tunneling is best illustrated through pitch overlays and because Baseball Savant has video clips for each pitch I built a relatively simple video overlayer in Python using the Movie.py library. Here is a clip of the best tunneled pitch as seen by this model:

Freddy Peralta Changeup and Fourseam

These pitches are essentially the opposite of tunneled. They come from completely different release points 9 inches apart, and then cross paths around the Tunnel Point and end up within 2 inches at the Plate. The two pitches start far apart and then end up close together instead of starting close together and ending far apart.

I believe one reason the regression model opted for pitches with low plate distances is because pitches that end up far apart are much more likely to be balls and unappealing to swing at. To counteract this I tried adding an interaction term between PC2 and the pitch being in the strike zone. Sure enough the coefficient for PC2 was negative when the pitch was in the zone indicating that in this case more late break is better. This was a better result but still not entirely what I wanted as a good tunnel doesn’t necessarily have to end in the strike zone.

My next strategy was aggregating the z-scores of PC1 and PC2. Equally weighting and adding the overall separation and late break ensures that what is thought of as a traditional tunnel, two pitches that look exactly the same for the beginning of their flight before aggressively breaking away from each other, was rewarded. This strategy was much more successful and generated some really good overlays:

Zach Wheeler Sinker and Fourseam

This was the highest scored tunnel in the dataset. Wheeler’s 97mph fourseam and 96mph sinker come from the exact same release point, are 0.18 inches apart at 40 feet and 2 inches apart at the tunnel point. After the tunnel point the fourseam stays on plane while the sinker drops ending up 8.5 inches below the fourseam. It’s hard to see in the video but the batter Martin Maldonato takes the first sinker for a strike and then likely identifies the following fourseam as the same exact pitch causing him to swing under it, illustrating the effectiveness of tunneling.

Brock Stewart Fourseam and Cutter

The first pitch in this sequence was a dotted 96mph fastball that should have been called a strike but unfortunately was a ball. The next pitch, a 92mph cutter, once again comes from the exact same release point and is within 0.3 inches at 40 feet and 2 inches at the tunnel point before cutting in and ending up lower and closer to the middle of the plate. This difference combined with a 4mph velocity difference results in another swing and miss.

Adrian Houser Sinker and Slider

My favorite overlay from the highest rated tunnels is Adrian Houser’s 90mph sinker and 82mph slider. These pitches come from the same release point, are within 0.1 inches at 40 feet, and are still within 4 inches at the tunnel point. Then the sinker darts inside to the righty batter while the slider sweeps outside ending 17 inches apart. Even though batter Jared Triolo impressively manages to lay off the low and away slider, this sequence makes for an incredible overlay.

Does Tunneling Matter:

In addition to passing the eye test this tunneling metric was also shown to impact the game itself. Good tunneling was associated with a higher probability of a swing and miss or called strike, lower quality contact, and run prevention. 

For individual pitches, a one standard deviation increase in Tunnel Score (10 point increase in Tunnel+) was associated with:

  • A 10.8% increase in swing and miss probability 

  • A 3.7% increase in called strike probability

  • A 0.005 point decrease in Expected wOBA on contact (xwOBAcon)

  • Preventing 0.006 more runs

While 0.005 points of xwOBAcon and 0.006 runs may seem small, it is important to remember that these values apply to each individual pitch. Over the course of a full season where a starting pitcher throws around 2,500 pitches, a 0.005 point drop in xwOBAcon is highly significant. Furthermore, preventing 0.006 runs per pitch equates to preventing an extra 15 runs over a season. Since roughly 9 to 10 runs equals 1 Win Above Replacement (WAR), a 1-standard-deviation increase in Tunnel+ is worth roughly 1.5 to 1.6 WAR.

I also applied this same tunnel metric to pitches from the first week of the 2026 MLB season. Good tunneling was once again associated with a higher probability of a swing and miss or a called strike, lower expected wOBA on contact, and run prevention. Even though the much smaller sample size resulted in the run prevention coefficient being only marginally significant (p=0.0894) and the xwOBAcon coefficient not reaching statistical significance, the directional stability of the coefficients is highly encouraging. It demonstrates that across multiple years, tunneling is a meaningfully positive attribute for a pitcher.

Pitcher Arsenals

Next I scored each pitcher’s overall arsenal by calculating a weighted average of each of their sequences and usage. Here are some of the standouts:

Tyler Rogers

Tyler Rogers had the highest Tunnel+ of 113. With a fastball averaging 83.2 mph (in the 1st percentile) Rogers relies primarily on deception to get hitters out. As a result it is unsurprising that he grades out very well in tunneling. What he lacks in velocity, especially for a reliever, he makes up for due to his unusual submarine armslot, unusual pitch shapes, and elite tunneling. Although it is worth noting that Rogers only throws two pitches giving him the advantage of every pitch in his small arsenal tunneling extremely well.

Bailey Ober

The second best Tunneler and the best tunneling starting pitcher with a Tunnel+ of 112 was Bailey Ober. Initially I was a little surprised, but after looking at Ober’s profile it definitely makes sense. Ober is another pitcher that was able to be very effective despite low fastball velocity. In 2024 he posted 91st percentile pitching run value, with a fastball that averaged 91.7mph (16th percentile) and the next year would decline to 90.3mph (6th percentile). Lacking velocity, Ober relied on elite offspeeds and tunneling. His slider and fastball had a Tunnel+ of 113 and changeup and fastball had a Tunnel+ of 110.

Degrom and Alcantara

The two big names in the top 10 are Jacob Degrom and Sandy Alcantara. These two pitchers are pretty much the opposite of Rogers and Ober. They are both power pitchers who pair elite velocity and elite tunneling to put up Cy Young winning numbers. Alcantara’s 98mph sinker and 92mph changeup are almost identical until the hitter’s decision point. Then the changeup drops off the table while the sinker keeps riding up and armside. This allowed Alcantara to generate extremely high weak contact rates early in counts which resulted in his historic volume. In addition to Alcantara’s sinker and changeup combo which has a Tunnel+ of 114, his sinker also pairs exceptionally well with his slider with a Tunnel+ of 112. This fastball slider combo is Jacob Degrom’s bread and butter. His 99mph fastball with elite carry and 92mph slider with sharp late break pair perfectly and had a Tunnel+ of 113. Even post elbow surgery where Degrom’s stuff hasn’t fully reached 2021 levels his elite command and tunneling allow him to remain one of the best pitchers in the league. 

Max Fried (The Anti-Tunneler)

As far as the worst tunnelers in the league, one in particular stood out: perennial Cy Young candidate Max Fried. His well below league average Tunnel+ of 85 is not something you would expect for a pitcher of his caliber. This just goes to show that there are multiple ways to be an effective pitcher. Max Fried’s arsenal relies on having a lot of high quality pitches. He throws a fourseam, cutter, sinker, two-seam, curveball, sweeper, and change-up all more than 10% of the time. His fastballs tunnel decently well with his fourseam and sinker having a Tunnel+ of 103. However none of his offspeeds tunnel very well. This is exacerbated by Fried’s most used offspeed pitch being his relatively loopy curveball which is a pitch that is more difficult to tunnel due to it tending to pop-up out of the hand. Many pitchers that are known for their curveballs are below league average at tunneling, for example Clayton Kershaw had a Tunnel+ of 94 even with the data being from later in his career where he had to rely more on tunneling. Despite not being an elite tunneler Fried is still an elite pitcher due to having a deadly arsenal of high stuff pitches.

My Own Tunneling:

As a college pitcher, I thought it would be interesting to evaluate how well my pitches tunnel together. My school has a Rapsodo which we use to collect data during bullpen sessions, so I was able to use that data to calculate the metrics needed to find my own Tunnel+ values. I used release point, velocity, and horizontal and vertical break data to determine the trajectory of the pitch and calculate positions at 40 feet, the tunnel point, and the plate.

One of the biggest challenges with this approach was the quality of the tracking data. Rapsodo is much less precise than the Hawkeye system used in Major League Baseball, particularly when measuring release points. Before each bullpen, the only calibration we perform is setting the unit at the correct distance from home plate. We do our best to center the Rapsodo on the plate and align it parallel to home plate, but small setup errors are unavoidable. While these inaccuracies have little effect on the velocity and pitch movement data that we typically analyze, they can significantly impact the horizontal release point (“release side”). As a result, each bullpen session in my dataset had a noticeably different average release side depending on how accurately the Rapsodo was aligned. 

I attempted to combat this through normalizing each session by applying a linear shift to each session, standardizing my mean release side to 2.6 feet (first-base side), which aligns with my true delivery. However, even after applying the linear shift many of the sessions still had outlier release points that didn’t seem reasonable and larger spreads than expected. This led me to conclude that portable Rapsodo tracking simply lacks the exactness required for precise release-point modeling. Because of this, my confidence in the exact Tunnel+ outputs for these bullpens is relatively low.

Even with those limitations, looking at overall trends was interesting. My best tunneling pitches seem to be my Curveball/Changeup and Curveball/Slider. This makes sense because these pitches have similar movement. Especially in bullpens where I’m not throwing my hardest, the movement profiles of these three pitches tend to blend together. 

The relationship I was most interested in was which pitch tunneled best with my fastball. It seemed to be my slider which is what I expected and affirms how I tend to use it. One of my favorite sequences against a right-handed batter is a fastball that starts inside and finishes over the middle of the plate, followed by a slider that begins on nearly the same trajectory before breaking farther inside. This has been a pretty effective sequence for me and due to the two pitches having a similar initial flight path before breaking apart, the slider often induces a swing-and-miss or weak contact.

While looking at my Rapsodo data was really cool, the lower quality data and small sample size (I only had 118 total pitch pairs, 76 of which were fastball slider pairs) made it hard to get a clear view of my own tunneling and how I compare to major league pitchers. In the future I’m going to attempt to get more and better data to see if I can learn anything more about myself as a player.

Limitations and Future Work:

The Constant Acceleration Assumption

The spatial checkpoints used in this model (such as the pitch location at 40 feet and the tunnel point) were calculated using basic kinematics derived from Statcast’s standard 9-parameter tracking data. This relies on the assumption of constant acceleration. As a result, it cannot fully account for non-linear aerodynamic forces such drag and Seam-Shifted Wake (SSW) that can alter a pitch’s trajectory. A future, more precise iteration of this model would utilize frame-by-frame optical tracking data rather than derived physics equations.

The Batter’s Visual Perspective

Tunnel+ evaluates the absolute 2D distance between pitches at specific spatial checkpoints. However, in reality, a tunnel is a matter of visual perception rather than absolute geometry. Because a batter stands offsetted in the batter’s box, their view of the pitch plane is angled. A two-inch horizontal separation between two pitches presents a completely different visual overlap to a left-handed hitter than a right-handed hitter. Future work could involve projecting these 2D coordinates into a true visual angle based on the batter’s specific eye level and handedness, measuring the deception rather than raw distance.

Strategic Intent

One of the downsides of evaluating every pitch sequence and aggregating them all into a single Tunnel+ score is that the model assumes that every pitch is meant to tunnel off of the previous one. In actual baseball strategy, this is not the case. Pitchers frequently utilize “contrast pitches” such as throwing a high four-seam fastball immediately after a low curveball specifically to change a batter’s eye level and disrupt their visual baseline. Because the metric evaluates spatial separation, these intentionally different sequences grade out as poor tunnels, which can artificially drag down the aggregate score of pitchers who heavily utilize eye-level shifts in their pitch calling. Future iterations could involve filtering out these purposefully non-tunneling sequences before applying a universal tunneling score.

Follow-Up Using Full 2026 Data

Data from the first week of the 2026 season was used to test whether the impact of Tunnel+ on an individual pitch’s success persists across multiple years. A similar follow-up analysis will be conducted to determine whether a pitcher’s Tunnel+ in prior datasets can accurately predict their 2026 Tunnel+.
